Monty Hall

Three doors. One spaceship. Two goats. The host knows what’s behind every door, and always opens a remaining goat door. Then you stay or switch.

Why this fooled everyone

In 1990, Marilyn vos Savant’s Parade column said you should switch. She received about 10,000 letters, many disagreeing.

Pick a door.

The host always opens a remaining goat door.

If you always switch, how often do you win?

Real game: the host knows, and always opens a goat door.

Switching wins 2 in 3

Your first pick is right only 1/3 of the time. Switching takes the other 2/3.

“The way that we obtain information is no less important than the information itself.”— Judea Pearl & Dana Mackenzie, The Book of Why

Why?

The other two doors together hold the spaceship 2/3 of the time. The host, who knows, folds those two doors into one by always revealing a goat.

Door Opened is a collider of Your Door and Location of Spaceship. Conditioning on what the host opened creates a new dependence — information that was not there before.

In the real game, simulate and you’ll see switch ≈ 2/3 and stay ≈ 1/3.

TakeawayHow the host generated the clue matters as much as the clue. A knowing host makes switching twice as good as staying; a random host does not.

Berkson’s paradox

Two fair coins, flipped independently. We’ll do this 100 times — but only write down a trial if at least one coin lands heads (~75 entries; every TT is thrown out).

Why?

This is the same structure as a hospital study that only includes people who got admitted for one disease or another.

In the list we kept, do Coin 1 and Coin 2 look independent?

The coins never influence each other when flipped. We’re asking only about the recorded outcomes — after every TT trial is deleted.

Flipping…

Whenever Coin 1 is tails, Coin 2 is heads — every single time in the recorded list. (The missing TT trials are why.)

Recording is a collider

“At least one heads” is caused by Coin 1 or Coin 2. Selecting on that collider manufactures a dependence that was never in the coins.

Why? Sackett’s hospital data

David Sackett (1979) saw the same pattern in hospital data. In the general population, disease of the bones and organs of movement occurred in 7.6% of people with respiratory disease vs. 7.2% without — essentially no association. Among people hospitalized in the prior 6 months, the rate was 25% (5 of 20) in those with respiratory disease vs. 7.6% in those without.

TakeawaySelecting on a collider — a hospital ward, a “noteworthy” case — can create a correlation that does not exist in the population.

Sex is decided before treatment: it affects both who tends to take the drug and who gets heart attacks. That makes sex a confounder.

Is Drug D a good or bad drug for heart attacks?

Bad for women, bad for men, bad for people

Women 5% → 7.5%. Men 30% → 40%. The totals (22% → 18%) look better only because more women took the drug, and women had fewer heart attacks either way.

Why? The adjusted rates

More women took the drug, and women had fewer heart attacks either way. Gender is a confounder of Drug and Heart Attack. Because men and women are equally frequent in the general population, average the two rates: 17.5% without Drug D (average of 5% and 30%) vs. 23.75% with it (average of 7.5% and 40%). Drug D is BBB: bad for women, bad for men, and bad for people.

Cells (Table 6.4): Female 1/20 vs 3/40; Male 12/40 vs 8/20; Total 13/60 vs 11/60.

TakeawayWhen the third variable is a confounder, stratify — then average. The totals here are misleading.

Is Drug B good or bad for heart attacks?

If you split on a mediator, you block part of the drug’s effect (the part that works by lowering blood pressure) and compare unlike leftover groups. The total effect is in the combined data: 18% vs. 22% heart attacks. Drug B helps.

TakeawaySame table, opposite moral. A confounder means stratify; a mediator means use the totals if you want the total effect.

Lord’s paradox

Two dining halls, two diets. Weighed in September and June. (Illustrative points, in the spirit of Wainer & Brown.)

Dashed line = no change. Overall gain in each hall is about zero — but in any vertical slice, Hall B ends higher.

Who is right?

First statistician: neither hall gained on average, so no diet effect. Second: at every starting weight, Hall B gained more.

If initial weight sends people to a hall, WI is a confounder of diet and final weight.

The diagram decides

If initial weight determines the hall, WI is a confounder. The second statistician is right.

Wainer & Brown — halls chosen (in part) by starting weight:

Why? Lord’s original (1967)

Lord compared boys and girls, not two diets. Sex influences initial weight, so WI is a mediator on the path from sex to final weight. Adjusting for it blocks part of the story; the first statistician — who notes that neither sex’s average weight changed — is right about the total change.

TakeawayThe same scatterplot can justify opposite conclusions. Whether WI is a confounder or a mediator is a causal question, not a statistical one.
