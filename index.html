Round 1 of 4

Monty Hall

Three doors. A car is behind one; goats behind the other two. You pick a door. The host, who knows what’s behind every door, always opens a different door that has a goat. Then you may stay or switch.

In 1990, Marilyn vos Savant’s Parade column said you should switch. She received about 10,000 letters, many disagreeing.

Round 1 · Monty Hall

Pick a door.

The host always opens a remaining goat door.

Round 1 · Your guess

If you always switch, how often do you win?

This is the real game: the host knows where the car is and always opens a goat door.

Round 1 · Reveal

Switching wins 2 in 3

Your first pick is right only 1/3 of the time. The other two doors together hold the car 2/3 of the time. The host, who knows, folds those two doors into one by always revealing a goat. Switching takes that entire 2/3.

Door Opened is a collider of Your Door and Location of Car. Conditioning on what the host opened creates a new dependence — information that was not there before.

“The way that we obtain information is no less important than the information itself.”— Judea Pearl & Dana Mackenzie, The Book of Why

In the real game, simulate and you’ll see switch ≈ 2/3 and stay ≈ 1/3.

TakeawayHow the host generated the clue matters as much as the clue. A knowing host makes switching twice as good as staying; a random host does not.
Round 2 of 4

Berkson’s paradox

Two fair coins, flipped independently. We’ll do this 100 times — but we only write down a trial if at least one coin lands heads. That leaves about 75 recorded flips (the TT trials are thrown out).

This is the same structure as a hospital study that only includes people who got admitted for one disease or another.

Round 2 · Your guess

In the recorded list, are the coins still independent?

Each coin is fair, and the flips don’t influence each other. We just ignore any trial that came up tails-tails.

Round 2 · The list

H

Coin 2

Flipping…

Whenever Coin 1 is tails, Coin 2 is heads — every single time in the recorded list. (The missing TT trials are why.)

Round 2 · Reveal

Recording is a collider

“At least one heads” is caused by Coin 1 or Coin 2. Selecting on that collider manufactures a negative dependence that was never in the coins.

David Sackett (1979) saw the same pattern in hospital data. In the general population, disease of the bones and organs of movement occurred in 7.6% of people with respiratory disease vs. 7.2% without — essentially no association. Among people hospitalized in the prior 6 months, the rate was 25% (5 of 20) in those with respiratory disease vs. 7.6% in those without.

TakeawaySelecting on a collider — a hospital ward, a “noteworthy” case — can create a correlation that does not exist in the population.

Simpson’s paradox

A fictional trial of Drug D (Table 6.4 in the book). First, the combined numbers only:

Is Drug D a good or bad drug?

Commit a guess from the totals. You’ll see the breakdown next.

Round 3 · By sex

Worse for women, worse for men

No drugDrug D
HA / noneRateHA / noneRate
Female 1 / 191/20 = 5% 3 / 373/40 = 7.5%
Male 12 / 2812/40 = 30% 8 / 128/20 = 40%
Total 13 / 4713/60 = 22% 11 / 4911/60 = 18%

Women: 5% without the drug vs. 7.5% with it. Men: 30% vs. 40%. Yet the totals say 22% vs. 18%. More women took the drug, and women had fewer heart attacks either way.

Gender is a confounder of Drug and Heart Attack. Because men and women are equally frequent in the general population, average the two rates: 17.5% without Drug D (average of 5% and 30%) vs. 23.75% with it (average of 7.5% and 40%). Drug D is BBB: bad for women, bad for men, and bad for people.

TakeawayWhen the third variable is a confounder, stratify — then average. The totals here are misleading.
Round 3 · The twist

Now it’s Drug B

Table 6.6 uses exactly the same numbers, relabeled. Blood pressure is measured after treatment. Drug B is meant to lower blood pressure, and blood pressure can cause heart attacks — so blood pressure is a mediator, not a confounder.

No drugDrug B
HA / noneRateHA / noneRate
Low BP 1 / 195% 3 / 377.5%
High BP 12 / 2830% 8 / 1240%

Is Drug B good or bad for heart attacks?

If you split on a mediator, you block part of the drug’s effect (the part that works by lowering blood pressure) and compare unlike leftover groups. The total effect is in the combined data: 18% vs. 22% heart attacks. Drug B helps.

TakeawaySame table, opposite moral. A confounder means stratify; a mediator means use the totals if you want the total effect.

Round 4 of 4

Lord’s paradox

Two dining halls, two diets. Students are weighed in September and again in June. (Illustrative points, in the spirit of Wainer & Brown’s version of the paradox.)

Each dot is a student. The dashed line is “no weight change.” Hall A starts lighter; Hall B starts heavier. Overall gain in each hall is about zero — but in any vertical slice (same starting weight), Hall B ends higher.

Round 4 · Your guess

Who is right?

The first statistician says: neither hall’s students gained weight on average, so the diets have no effect. The second says: at every initial weight, Hall B gained more, so Hall B’s diet causes extra gain.

Suppose initial weight is what sends people to a hall — lighter students tend to choose Hall A, heavier students Hall B. Then initial weight (WI) is a confounder of diet and final weight.

Round 4 · Reveal

The diagram decides

If initial weight determines which diet hall a student enters, WI is a confounder. The second statistician, who stratifies by initial weight, is right.

Wainer & Brown version — diet halls chosen (in part) by starting weight:

Lord’s original (1967) compared boys and girls, not two diets. Sex influences initial weight, so WI is a mediator on the path from sex to final weight. Adjusting for it blocks part of the story; the first statistician — who notes that neither sex’s average weight changed — is right about the total change.

TakeawayThe same scatterplot can justify opposite conclusions. Whether WI is a confounder or a mediator is a causal question, not a statistical one.
